who will build the AWS of office space?

This is the refinement of an idea that I had originally titled “what would a WeWork of the future look like?”

That direction was deeply flawed — I realized, too late — due in large part to the fact that WeWork = bad.

Duh. Oops. My grand "remote work future vision" flopped.

It was only in the final moments of penning that post that I noticed a parallel to cloud computing, and scrawled in a hasty mention. It turns out to be the far better metaphor.

Mercifully, my submission did merit one comment, which did precipitate an illuminating exchange, which...did make it all totally worth it. Shoutout gregjor — may you find hope for the future!

To quote myself:

“Think of it like AWS. Each company rents only as many spaces as they need in that area. There would be employees of hundreds of companies working out of the same building. There might be a small Facebook team, an individual Microsoft employee, people from random startups, whatever. Hiring someone in Alberta? See if you can requisition a space in a nearby center. Don't need to build your own entire office or have that person move or work from home.”

At that point it started to become less of an idea and more of a self-fulfilling prophecy (in my mind). As the segment of remote-capable jobs grows, and candidate pools span larger swathes of the globe, office-space-as-a-service seems an inevitable optimization.

background

The initial assumption for this idea was the observation (not mine) that the continued shift from agricultural and manufacturing jobs to “information work” is driving individuals and businesses towards city centers in a runaway feedback loop. Urban infrastructures are being strained, affordable housing is in crisis, and more outlying areas are becoming underutilized.

Information work is, naturally, substantial economic activity that can take place anywhere. How can we turn this into a win for everyone?

So glad you asked! Right now, we're calling it the Remote Work Campus (RWC).

RWC?

Philosophically, somewhere between a tech campus and a university campus. Geographically, in the sweet spot between the city center and the sticks. Operationally, commoditizing the Google HQ experience with fine-grained multitenancy. Requisition and decommission office units worldwide with a few clicks.

Many smaller independent units, more like an apartment building than a typical tech office floor. Coworking at enterprise scale.

Extensive integrated amenities — convenient and quality food options, gym, day care, supply stores, auto care, walking trails, etc. Pricing model TBD but it's important to deliver here. Costs can be offset by efficiencies gained in other areas.

I had a goofy idea to emulate the immediacy of being near your team, with built-in videoconferencing screens and control hardware, but that may not be necessary. Maybe R&D it for a future evolution. Starting out with decent rooms with windows and a couple cool gizmos would probably be sufficient.

(The reader may suggest that many multi-tenant office buildings exist already — and see the PS section for further comment on prior art — but they generally don't deliver the same experience. They're often largely siloed and subject to the tragedy of the commons. The RWC distributes and obscures company territories and boundaries, buckets people more by where they live, and supports the commons via central mandate).

culture

One very interesting property of the RWC is that it shifts the dominant culture of the campus from the company to the community. The basic economic unit and primary satisfaction target moves closer to the individual.

Within your office, you work for your employer, but you step out into a more communal space where you might have lunch with all sorts of people. Less company town Kool-Aid vibes. More civic center, in that the core commonality between everyone there is where they live, not who they work for.

The work environment provider is decoupled from the employer. It becomes a separate business that competes on a different plane, with its own goals and stakeholders. It has the opportunity to focus solely on creating nice places to work, and making them available.

This aspect might not be immediately attractive to extant big companies that have already invested heavily in their HQs, but for everyone else, particularly the burgeoning remote-native guard, it should be a clear win.

Some other bullet points:

good for employees

  • Get the quality of life of a modern tech campus
  • Don’t have to move away from your home area
  • Work amongst and socialize with a swath of your local community
  • Have a dedicated, modern, private workspace
  • More smaller office units with doors, less huge open floorplans
  • Change jobs with zero disruption, even stay at the same desk
  • Conversely, move your family and keep the same job

good for employers

  • Save huge amounts of time and resources on lengthy real estate and construction projects (this is “on-prem”)
  • Efficiently adapt to changes in need
  • Global availability
  • Maintain sense of office culture, teamwork and camaraderie
  • Remote productivity boosters

good for the planet

  • Leverage existing underutilized infrastructure
  • Reduce commuting and traffic
  • Reduce strain on urban centers where expansion is difficult and costly
  • Reduce construction churn, facilities managed independently of tenant turnover
  • Move the company to the people, not the people to the company
  • Revitalize areas closer to nature

key differentiators

  • Modular - Operates more at the individual level than traditional office leasing
  • Fungible - effortless turnover, self-serve
  • Experiential - provide impressive value with campus amenities
  • Innovative - Built-in video comms tech

conclusion

A few days ago, I woke up, had an idea, started writing, dusted off my website for the first time in years, added the writing section I'd long dreamt of, published my first piece, and submitted it to HN. All within a matter of hours.

The next day, whilst showering, I thought, “why did I do that?”

After all, I have lots of ideas and have written lots of drafts and have always "wanted" to publish them, but this was the one that broke through.

And then I recalled that I used to feel like I had never had a truly winning, IPO-grade startup idea. Subsequently, my interests drifted away from such things.

But I feel this one in my bones! And I want to see it. But I'm not the one to do it. So it’s up for grabs. If it’s you, maybe just bring me on a bit. ;)

If it's not, tell me why I’m wrong on HN!

ps

A note on “serviced offices” and the “flexible workspace industry” (e.g. Regus):

I had never heard of these, and I think that says enough. Definitely precursors to the RWC, but whether the time wasn't right, or the vision wasn't there, these are the phones that existed before the iPhone. The opportunity has not yet been realized.

It needs to be Amazon’d. The inefficiencies need to be attacked, it needs to be relentlessly customer experience driven. Make a place people want to be. Make a place that’s just better than anything else.

(And we have the blueprints! Places like this exist, they’re just not accessible to most.)
