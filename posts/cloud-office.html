who will build the AWS of office space?

This is the refinement of an idea that I had originally titled “what would a WeWork of the future look like?”

That direction was deeply flawed — I realized, too late — due in large part to the fact that WeWork = bad.

Duh. Oops. My grand "remote work future vision" flopped.

It was only in the final moments of penning that post that I noticed a parallel to cloud computing, and scrawled in a hasty mention. It turns out to be the far better metaphor.

Mercifully, my submission did merit one comment, which did precipitate an illuminating exchange, which...did make it all totally worth it. Shoutout gregjor — may you find hope for the future!

To quote myself:

“Think of it like AWS. Each company rents only as many spaces as they need in that area. There would be employees of hundreds of companies working out of the same building. There might be a small Facebook team, an individual Microsoft employee, people from random startups, whatever. Hiring someone in Alberta? See if you can requisition a space in a nearby center. Don't need to build your own entire office or have that person move or work from home.”

At that point it started to become less of an idea and more of a self-fulfilling prophecy (in my mind). As the segment of remote-capable jobs grows, and candidate pools span larger swathes of the globe, office-space-as-a-service seems an inevitable optimization.

background

THe catalyst for this idea was the observation (not mine) that the continued shift from agricultural and manufacturing jobs to “information work” is driving individuals and businesses towards city centers in a runaway feedback loop. Urban infrastructures are being strained, affordable housing is in crisis, and more outlying areas are becoming underutilized.

Information work is, naturally, substantial economic activity that can take place anywhere. How can we turn this into a win for everyone?

So glad you asked!

Right now we're calling it the Community Work Campus (CWC).

CWC?

Philosophically, between a tech campus and a university campus. Geographically, in the sweet spot between the city center and the sticks. Operationally, commoditizing the Google HQ experience with self-serve fine-grained multitenancy. Requisition and decommission office units worldwide with ease.

Many smaller independent units, more like an apartment building than a typical tech office floor. A high degree of fungibility, fast turnover times, less custom remodeling. Some size and usage options.

Extensive integrated amenities — convenient and quality food options, gym, day care, supply stores, auto care, walking trails, etc. Pricing model TBD but it's important to to deliver on this. Offset costs with efficiencies gained in other areas.

I had a goofy idea involving built-in videoconferencing screens and control hardware to emulate the immediacy of being near your team, but that could unnecessary or a boondoggle. R&D it for later. Decent rooms with windows and a couple cool gizmos would probably be sufficient to start.

(The reader may suggest that many multitenant office buildings exist — and see the PS section for further comment on prior art — but they generally operate the "on-prem" model. Big upfront costs and commitments to lease and furnish the spaces, inelastic, highly siloed, weak shared amenities if any. The CWC distributes and obscures company territories and boundaries, pools people more by where they live, and centrally manages the greater experience. Smaller tenants that don't do huge custom build outs would see this a strict upgrade over the typical suburban office park. Part of the value is in making use of and revamping that existing infrastructure that is in declining demand. It wouldn't just be remote workers either. Law offices, accountants, artist studios, and the like would be equally at home here.)

culture

One interesting property of the CWC is that it shifts the dominant culture of the campus from the company to the community. The basic economic unit and primary satisfaction target moves closer to the individual.

Within your office, you work for your employer, but you step out into a more communal space where you might have lunch with all sorts of people. Less company town, more civic center. The common unifying thread between everyone there is where they live, not who they work for.

The work environment provider is decoupled from the employer. It becomes a separate business with its own goals and stakeholders. It has the opportunity to focus on creating nice places to work, competing on that.

This aspect might not be immediately attractive to extant big companies that have already invested heavily in their HQs, but to everyone else it should be a clear win.

Some bullet points:

good for employees

  • Get the quality of life of a modern tech campus
  • Don’t have to move away from your home area
  • Work amongst and socialize with a swath of your local community
  • Have a dedicated, modern, private workspace
  • More smaller office units with doors, less huge open floorplans
  • Change jobs with zero disruption, even stay at the same desk
  • Conversely, move your family and keep the same job

good for employers

  • Save huge amounts of time and resources on lengthy real estate and construction projects
  • Efficiently adapt to changes in need
  • Global availability
  • Maintain sense of office culture, teamwork and camaraderie
  • Remote productivity boosters

good for the planet

  • Leverage existing underutilized infrastructure
  • Reduce commuting and traffic
  • Reduce strain on urban centers where expansion is difficult and costly
  • Reduce construction churn, facilities managed independently of tenant turnover
  • Move the company to the people, not the people to the company
  • Revitalize areas closer to nature

key differentiators

  • Modular - Operates more at the individual level than traditional office leasing
  • Fungible - effortless turnover, self-serve
  • Experiential - provide impressive value with campus amenities
  • Innovative - Built-in video comms tech, integrated services

conclusion

A few days ago, I woke up, had an idea, started writing, dusted off my website for the first time in years, added the writing section I'd long dreamt of, published my first piece, and submitted it to HN. All within a matter of hours.

The next day, whilst showering, I thought, “why did I do that?”

After all, I have lots of ideas and have written lots of drafts and have always "wanted" to publish them, but this was the one that broke through, out of nowhere.

And then I recalled that I used to feel like I had never had a truly winning, IPO-grade startup idea. Subsequently, my interests drifted away from such things.

But I feel this one in my bones, and I want to see it. But I'm not the one to do it. So it’s up for grabs.

If it’s you, maybe just bring me on. ;)

If not, tell me why I’m wrong on HN!

ps

A note on “serviced offices” and the “flexible workspace industry” (e.g. Regus):

I had never heard of these, and I think that says enough. Definitely precursors to the RWC, but whether the time wasn't right, or the vision wasn't there, these are the phones that existed before the iPhone. The opportunity has yet to be actualized.

It has to be Amazon’d. The inefficiencies need to be attacked to drive a better customer experience. Make places people want to be. Make places that are just better.

And we have the playbook. Places like this exist, they’re just not accessible to most.
